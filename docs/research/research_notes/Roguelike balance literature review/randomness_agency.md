# Randomness, Variance and Player Agency in Single-Player Roguelikes

Method note: direct page fetches were blocked by the sandbox network policy (WebFetch DNS failures; proxy 403 on CONNECT), so every finding below comes from search-engine result summaries of the cited pages, not from reading the full text. Quotes in double quotes are wording that appeared in those summaries; treat them as near-verbatim, not checked against the original. "Research" = peer-reviewed or empirical study; "Practitioner opinion" = designer talks, books, blogs. Where a source is secondary (wiki, review, summary site) it is labelled.

Context for applying this: Dungeon Scramble's per-turn 4x4 dice roll is **input randomness** (it resolves before the player chooses a path). Enemy/boss attacks shown in advance are **telegraphed information**. Loot dice, relics and rewards are **between-fight input randomness**. Any hidden roll after the player commits (e.g., a proc chance, a miss chance) would be **output randomness**.

## 1. Input randomness vs output randomness: which works where, and what dice games do

### Takeaway
Designers mostly agree that input randomness (random state shown *before* the decision) keeps decisions meaningful while output randomness (random result *after* commitment) blurs feedback. But the one controlled study found the reverse for card draws, and dice games such as Slice & Dice and Dicey Dungeons make heavy output randomness feel fair by letting players lock, reroll or change dice. For Dungeon Scramble, the roll-then-plan structure is already the "good" kind. The risk is a roll with no viable path, which is input randomness without enough choice.

### Cited Findings
- **Definitions (practitioner consensus).** Input randomness resolves before the player decides; output randomness resolves after. One framing: "what does the player know and when do they know it." Examples: drawing a hand in Slay the Spire (input) vs rolling dice in Risk (output). — [Loop & Ledger, "Input vs output randomness"](https://loopandledger.beehiiv.com/p/input-vs-output-randomness); [Skeleton Code Machine, "Input-Output Randomness (Part 1)"](https://www.skeletoncodemachine.com/p/input-output-randomness-part-1)
- **Who coined the terms is disputed.** One source credits Geoff Engelstein; another credits the Ludology podcast (which Engelstein co-hosted, so these may be the same origin). — [Skeleton Code Machine](https://www.skeletoncodemachine.com/p/input-output-randomness-part-1); [Keith Burgun, "Riot is thinking about input/output randomness"](https://keithburgun.net/riot-is-thinking-about-inputoutput-randomness-in-league-of-legends)
- **Keith Burgun (opinion, strong form).** In a 2014 Game Developer essay he argues "the current assumptions about randomness in strategy games are largely wrong" and that output randomness (noise between choice and result) does not belong in a strategy game. His book *Clockwork Game Design* (Routledge, 2015) builds this into a method: start from a strong core and remove whatever conflicts with it. — [Burgun, "Randomness and Game Design," Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/randomness-and-game-design); [Clockwork Game Design, O'Reilly listing](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/-/9781138798731/)
- **Why output randomness is criticised (secondhand summary of Burgun).** A player can play well and still lose to a bad roll, which muddies feedback and makes learning harder. Critics also say it adds apparent depth without real depth. — [UFJF InovaGames (Portuguese summary)](https://www2.ufjf.br/inovagames/2025/12/06/aleatoriedade-nos-jogos-ferramenta-estrategica-ou-ilusao-de-profundidade/)
- **The categories are a continuum.** Badly handled input randomness, such as giving the player too little time or too few options to react, causes the same problems as output randomness. — [Game Developer, "Randomness and Game Design"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/randomness-and-game-design)
- **Input randomness needs real choices to be well received.** — [Level99 "Guidelines for Strategy Game Design: Input/Output Randomness"](https://level99store.com/blogs/guidelines/3-5-1-3-5-2-input-output-randomness)
- **RESEARCH, and it contradicts the consensus.** Zhang, Monteiro, Liang, Ma & Baghaei (2021, arXiv 2107.08437; also in IEEE conference proceedings) built a custom collectable card game with four input/output randomness conditions. They found that input randomness (random card draws) lowered player satisfaction more than output randomness (random card effects). A small lab study in one genre, but the only controlled test of the distinction I found. — [arXiv 2107.08437](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.08437); [XJTLU scholar listing](https://scholar.xjtlu.edu.cn/en/publications/effect-of-input-output-randomness-on-gameplay-satisfaction-in-col/)
- **How dice roguelikes make output randomness fair (practitioner analysis).** Slice & Dice has "a LOT of output randomness" yet feels fair because players can influence the odds: they can "lock" dice to keep results, so a risky last reroll comes only after giving up a guaranteed medium result. The physical-looking on-screen dice also help. The same piece quotes Zach Gage saying that seeing dice "cracks the problem that humans have with understanding odds". — [Ezra Szanton, "Output randomness & game designers"](https://ezraszanton.substack.com/p/output-randomness-game-designers). Attribution caveat: Slice & Dice is usually credited to the developer "tann", not Gage. The search summary may have run two sections of the essay together, so verify which game the Gage quote is about.
- **Dicey Dungeons (Terry Cavanagh).** Cavanagh said random cards combined with random dice felt "way too chaotic", so most characters use static equipment layouts (the Jester is the exception). Each character's tools for bending dice (the Warrior's rerolls, the Thief's lockpick splitting dice) are how runs get variety and how players fight the RNG. Reviews split: one says "a few unlucky rolls can end a run quickly"; another says "the numbers you're handed every round are random, what you do with them... is entirely in your control." — [Game Developer, Road to the IGF: Dicey Dungeons](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/disciplines/road-to-the-igf-cavanagh-houston-dobbe-s-i-dicey-dungeons-i-); [GamingTrend review](https://gamingtrend.com/reviews/of-dice-and-decks-dicey-dungeons-review/); [AV Club](https://avclub.com/gambling-s-a-hell-of-a-lot-more-fun-with-dice-that-can-1837692384)
- **Into the Breach (Subset Games) as the low-randomness extreme.** The team wanted a game with less randomness than FTL and telegraphed enemy attacks as a core mechanic, aiming for "every death felt like your own fault." Telegraphs turn each turn into "a kind of puzzle." A review notes "all actionable information is laid bare," with "almost no element of randomness beyond a grid defense chance." The difficulty moves into tradeoffs, such as dodging an attack only to have a more important building hit. — [Game Developer, Road to the IGF: Into the Breach](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-subset-games-i-into-the-breach-i-); [Nintendo Life review](https://nintendolife.com/reviews/switch-eshop/into_the_breach)
- **Garfield et al. on luck and skill.** *Characteristics of Games* (Elias, Garfield, Gutschera; MIT Press 2012, paperback 2020) treats "degrees of luck and skill needed" as one of its core axes for comparing games. — [MIT Press Bookstore listing](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780262542692)

### Inferences
- Dungeon Scramble's core loop (roll the tray, then plan a path, then resolve deterministically) is input randomness plus deterministic resolution. That is the structure Burgun and the Into the Breach team favour, and boss telegraphs follow the Into the Breach model. The design-theory risk is not output noise but **degenerate inputs**: a tray where no path is good, which the Game Developer piece says behaves like output randomness. Swap/Reroll tray actions are the Slice & Dice/Dicey Dungeons fix, which supports keeping and possibly extending them.
- Zhang et al. suggest input randomness is not automatically satisfying. It probably satisfies only when the player has enough tools to act on it (this is my synthesis, not tested in the paper).
- Any hidden post-commit roll (e.g., a percentage proc on a relic) should be visible and influenceable, or be replaced with deterministic or counter-based triggers.

### Gaps
- Could not read Engelstein's own definition text (GameTek book or Ludology episodes) or the full Burgun essay; exact quotes are unverified.
- No direct quote from *Characteristics of Games* on luck vs skill was retrievable (e.g., its discussion of luck letting players of different skill compete, or short-term vs long-term luck). Check the book itself.
- Sample size and effect sizes for Zhang et al. not retrieved.

## 2. Variance and memorable runs: peak-end, broken combos, interesting decisions, story generation, mastery

### Takeaway
Memory of a play session is shaped by its peak and its end more than by its average (lab evidence for this in games is real but mixed). Single-player roguelikes can tolerate, and benefit from, rare overpowered combos because no opponent suffers from them. Meier's "interesting decisions" and Koster's "fun is learning" both imply that variance is valuable only while it creates new patterns to learn and real choices.

### Cited Findings
- **Peak-end rule, origin.** Redelmeier's colonoscopy studies (University of Toronto, 1990s): patients' remembered "total pain" tracked roughly the average of the worst moment and the final moment, and duration had almost no effect ("duration neglect"). Engelstein separates the "experiencing self" from the "remembering self" and applies this to board games. — [Engelstein, "Colonoscopies and Board Games," GameTek Substack, 16 Apr 2023](https://gametek.substack.com/p/colonoscopies-and-board-games)
- **RESEARCH: peak-end in games (Gutwin et al., CHI 2016, "Peak-End Effects on Player Experience in Casual Games").** Identical games (a Bejeweled-like, a reaction game, then a shootout) were played with the difficulty order manipulated to produce positive, negative or neutral peak-end. Recalled challenge was strongly shaped by peak-end. Ratings of fun, enjoyment and willingness to replay were less consistent: some were significant in the predicted direction, some not, none reversed. — [Gutwin et al., CHI 2016 PDF](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~jeromew/data/COMP766/CHI2016/p5608-gutwin.pdf); [DOI 10.1145/2858036.2858419](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858419)
- **RESEARCH: Hearthstone peak-end (2020, n=26).** Strong correlation between self-reported peak/end values and retrospective emotion, but not with facial-expression measures. — [Tilburg University, "Exploring Peak-end Effects in Player Affect Through Hearthstone"](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/exploring-peak-end-effects-in-player-affect-through-hearthstone/)
- **RESEARCH: difficulty pacing (67 students, FPS).** Difficulty curves with peaks had the strongest effect on motivation. — [HAL, "Difficulty Pacing Impact on Player Motivation"](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-03847459)
- **Broken combos in single-player (practitioner, Slay the Spire).** In the GDC 2019 talk "Slay the Spire: Metrics Driven Design and Balance," Anthony Giovannetti (Mega Crit) said being a single-player roguelike allows "rare overpowered combos without disrupting other players' experiences." Balance aimed for "every card and element had a purpose, not making everything equal." Metrics "can be misleading if not carefully interpreted." Ascension levels, weekly patches, Discord and streamer feedback shaped balance. — [Glasp talk summary](https://glasp.co/youtube/7rqfbvnO_H0); [GDC Vault listing](https://www.gdcvault.com/browse/gdc-19/play/1025731); [Game Developer preview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/learn-i-slay-the-spire-i-s-metrics-driven-approach-to-game-balancing-at-gdc-2019) (secondary summaries; Vault is members-only)
- **Sid Meier, "interesting decisions" (practitioner).** GDC 2012 talk "Interesting Decisions" revisits his 1989 line that "a game is a series of interesting decisions." Sources disagree on whether the original wording was "interesting decisions" or "meaningful choices." — [Game Developer video post](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/video-sid-meier-explores-interesting-decisions-in-gameplay); [GDC news](https://gdconf.com/news/gdc_vault_adds_free_gdc_2012_v); [Cato commentary](https://www.cato.org/commentary/reviewing-sid-meiers-lessons-game-design-civilization-wargames)
- **Common elaboration of the rule (as Juul summarises it):** no option should be consistently superior, options should not be equally attractive, and "the player must be able to make an informed choice." — [Jesper Juul, "Just what is it..."](https://jesperjuul.net/text/justwhatisit.html)
- **Tynan Sylvester (practitioner, RimWorld; *Designing Games*, O'Reilly 2013).** He discusses encouraging apophenia (players reading stories into systems) and advises "Choose the minimum representation that supports the kinds of stories you want to generate." RimWorld's semi-random events are paced by AI "storytellers," compared to Left 4 Dead's AI Director. A podcast covers his idea of "elastic failure" in story generation. — [Soton blog summary of Designing Games](https://generic.wordpress.soton.ac.uk/archaeology/2013/06/09/twitter-is-your-friend/); [GameSkinny interview](https://www.gameskinny.com/news/bioshock-infinite-dev-talks-about-his-kickstarter-rimworld-in-this-exclusive-interview/); [Game Maker's Notebook podcast](https://gamemakersnotebook.libsyn.com/generating-human-stories-with-rimworld-creator-tynan-sylvester)
- **Raph Koster, *A Theory of Fun* (practitioner).** Fun is the brain learning and mastering patterns. Once a pattern is mastered the game turns trivial and boring (tic-tac-toe is his example), and when it is too hard the player may not see the pattern at all. Unless patterns keep arriving in novel ways, boredom follows. Bogost criticises the focus on fun (*Unit Operations*, ch. 8). — [Game Studies Wiki](https://game-studies.fandom.com/wiki/A_Theory_of_Fun_for_Game_Design?oldid=4074); [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/blog/what-is-the-meaning-of-fun/) (secondary)

### Inferences
- Peak-end supports designing for highlight moments (a loot die that suddenly breaks a fight open, a huge path chain) and for strong endings: a satisfying boss kill, a run-end card that recaps peaks even on a loss. Gutwin et al. show that memory of challenge reliably follows peak-end, while fun ratings do so less reliably, so do not over-claim.
- Following Giovannetti, occasional broken loot/relic synergies are a feature in a solo roguelike. The balance target is "each item has a purpose," not equal power. Depth tiers can absorb the broken runs.
- Koster plus Meier: variance between runs (tray, loot, enemies) is what keeps patterns from going stale, but only while each roll still offers an informed, non-obvious choice.

### Gaps
- No primary text from *Designing Games* on randomness specifically was retrievable. "Elastic failure" and the storyteller pacing are second-hand.
- No empirical study found that measures "broken combo" moments or power fantasy in roguelikes directly.

## 3. Perceived fairness of luck: loss aversion, misjudged odds, fudging, pseudo-random distribution

### Takeaway
Players systematically misjudge probability and feel losses more than equivalent gains. Commercial games routinely bend the RNG toward the player through fudged displayed odds, streak breakers, pseudo-random distribution and hidden aim assist, so that true randomness does not feel rigged. Meier's position is that player psychology, not mathematical accuracy, should decide how odds behave.

### Cited Findings
- **Sid Meier, GDC 2010 keynote "The Psychology of Game Design (Everything You Know Is Wrong)" (practitioner, secondhand write-ups).**
  - Thesis: the driving force of design should be the player's psychology. — [GDC press release via UBM](https://ubmchannelnews.mediaroom.com/index.php?item=87798)
  - Civilization Revolution playtesters losing at 3:1 odds in their favour complained, but did not complain about winning at 3:1 against. They accepted losing at 2:1 but not at 20:10 (same ratio, larger numbers). The team changed the combat math so the experience felt fair. — [GameDev.net notes](https://gamedev.net/blogs/entry/2111213-the-psychology-of-game-design-sid-meier); [Game Informer, "Sid Meier: Gamers Aren't Logical"](https://gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2010/03/12/sid-meier-gamers-arent-logical)
  - "Winner paradox": players expect to win far more than the odds allow; he has never received mail complaining about winning too much. — [Museum of Play write-up](https://www.museumofplay.org/blog/gdc-2010-game-psychology-101/); [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2010/03/12/sid-meier-gamers-arent-logical)
  - Players accept unexplained rewards but treat unexplained punishments as a broken or cheating game, so explain why a punishment happens. — [GameDev.net notes](https://gamedev.net/blogs/entry/2111213-the-psychology-of-game-design-sid-meier)
  - Caveat: no slides or recording retrieved; numbers come from these write-ups.
- **Loss aversion in games (Engelstein, *Achievement Relocked: Loss Aversion and Game Design*, MIT Press 2020).** Losses hurt more than equal gains please, and designers can use that asymmetry. The book covers the endowment effect (Portal's Companion Cube), gain-vs-loss framing, Deal or No Deal and utility theory, regret and competence in legacy games, and Uwe Rosenberg's growing command of loss aversion. — [Penguin Random House listing](https://penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/654003/achievement-relocked-by-geoffrey-engelstein); [MIT Press Bookstore](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780262043533)
- **Misjudging streaks (low-to-medium quality sources).** People "dramatically underestimate the probability of hot or cold streaks"; the gambler's fallacy ("a win is due") and the hot-hand error are both common, and players treat dice as "cursed" after streaks despite knowing they have no memory. — [VASSAL forum](https://forum.vassalengine.org/t/dice/1602); [Free the Dice, "Psychology of Lucky Dice"](https://www.freethedice.com/yahtzees/psychology-of-lucky-dice-in-board-games.php). Low-quality sources; the underlying cognitive-bias literature (Kahneman & Tversky) is established but was not fetched here.
- **Anecdote showing perceived bias:** a Steam user logged 60 Dicey Dungeons Warrior rerolls and suspected rigging; other users called it confirmation bias. — [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/861540/discussions/0/1742260553311626215)
- **Pseudo-random distribution (Dota 2 / Warcraft III).** PRD replaces independent rolls "where it made sense for the developers to limit long streaks." A 25% bash starts at about 8.5% and rises by about 8.5% after each miss, resetting after a proc, so the long-run rate is still about 25% but long droughts and long streaks are rare. All chance-based crits use PRD. It originates in the Warcraft III engine, and exploiting it is "nearly impossible" in practice. — [Liquipedia, Random Distribution](https://liquipedia.net/dota2/Random_Number_Generation); [Dota 2 Fandom wiki, PRD](https://dota2.fandom.com/wiki/Pseudo-random_distribution); [Diplograph note](https://diplograph.net/notes/games/randomness/dota-2-prd) (community wikis, not official Valve docs)
- **Hidden aim assist (XCOM 2).** Fan and config-file sources describe a hidden bonus for shots above 50% after previous misses (config `MissStreakChanceAdjustment`, reported as 10 on Rookie/Veteran and 15 on Commander; none on Legendary), capped at 95%. UFOpaedia lists "significant hidden aim assist" on Rookie. Exact numbers conflict between sources. — [Steam discussion quoting DefaultGameCore.ini](https://steamcommunity.com/app/268500/discussions/0/412447331645399260); [Nexus mod page](https://www.nexusmods.com/xcom2/mods/212); [UFOpaedia Difficulty (XCOM2)](https://new.ufopaedia.org/Difficulty_(XCOM2))
- **Visible dice help odds comprehension:** "visually seeing dice cracks the problem that humans have with understanding odds" (attributed to Zach Gage). — [Ezra Szanton](https://ezraszanton.substack.com/p/output-randomness-game-designers)

### Inferences
- Bad-luck protection fits Dungeon Scramble's tray naturally. Examples: guarantee at least one viable path or attack face per roll; raise loot-die face weights after droughts (PRD-style counters); never deal two "dead" trays in a row. Following Meier and XCOM, scale the protection by depth (generous at low depth, none at the top), which mirrors XCOM's per-difficulty assist.
- Meier's punishment point maps onto the existing telegraphs and lethal warning. When the player takes big damage, the screen should show why (which telegraphed attack, which minion). Unexplained damage reads as cheating.
- Loss aversion: frame mitigation as gains ("+1 reroll"), not removals; persistent losses (losing an owned loot die) will feel much worse than the equivalent gain feels good.

### Gaps
- No official Firaxis/Valve source for the assist or PRD numbers; no primary Kahneman/Tversky citations fetched.
- No research found that compares PRD with true random on player-rated fairness.

## 4. Agency: learnable losses vs bad luck; learning from failure; mitigation tools

### Takeaway
Players endure and even seek failure when it reads as their own, fixable inadequacy (Juul). Competence and autonomy predict enjoyment (Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski). Telegraphs and perfect information (Into the Breach) and RNG-manipulation tools (Dicey Dungeons, Slice & Dice) are the main ways practitioners move a loss from "bad luck" to "my mistake." Permadeath research is thin: frustration rises, but how meaningful a death feels mediates whether players continue.

### Cited Findings
- **Jesper Juul, *The Art of Failure* (MIT Press 2013, academic essay).** The "paradox of failure": we seek out unpleasant experiences of failure in games. Failure stings because "you (not a character) are in some way inadequate." Games then motivate us to play on to escape that inadequacy, and escaping failure, often by improving skill, is central to their enjoyment. Games are "the singular art form that sets us up for failure and allows us to experience it and experiment with it." — [MIT CMS summary](https://cms.mit.edu/jesper-juul-the-pain-of-playing-video-games); [jesperjuul.net, About the book](https://jesperjuul.net/artoffailure/about.html)
- **RESEARCH: Self-determination theory (Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006, *Motivation and Emotion* 30(4):347-363).** Across four studies, in-game autonomy and competence were associated with enjoyment, preference and short-term well-being changes. Intuitive controls related to competence and autonomy. Ryan: the "pull" of games is "largely due to their capacity to engender feelings of autonomy, competence, and relatedness." This led to the PENS measure. — [ScienceDaily release](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/12/061226134706.htm); [Ewha pure record](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/the-motivational-pull-of-video-games-a-self-determination-theory-/); [SDT PENS page](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/player-experience-of-needs-satisfaction-pens/)
- **Into the Breach: "every death felt like your own fault"** through telegraphs and near-perfect information (see section 1). — [Game Developer IGF interview](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-subset-games-i-into-the-breach-i-)
- **Mitigation tools in dice roguelikes:** Dicey Dungeons gives each character its own dice-manipulation identity (rerolls, splitting dice); Slice & Dice uses lock-and-reroll; one reviewer still reports "a few unlucky rolls can end a run quickly." — [Game Developer IGF interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/disciplines/road-to-the-igf-cavanagh-houston-dobbe-s-i-dicey-dungeons-i-); [GamingTrend](https://gamingtrend.com/reviews/of-dice-and-decks-dicey-dungeons-review/); [Ezra Szanton](https://ezraszanton.substack.com/p/output-randomness-game-designers)
- **Informed choice as a precondition of an interesting decision** ("the player must be able to make an informed choice"). — [Juul](https://jesperjuul.net/text/justwhatisit.html)
- **RESEARCH (thesis-level, small samples): permadeath.**
  - Don't Starve Together study (n=65): permadeath significantly increased frustration at death, but the *meaning attributed to death* mediated the link between frustration and willingness to keep playing, so frustration did not directly reduce continuation. Framed with Player-Avatar Relationship theory. — [NCCU thesis](https://thesis.lib.nccu.edu.tw/thesis/detail/b6a7e5c99b153b3ae67e5b7ab41e1f11)
  - TCD 2014 dissertation: an experimental roguelike with and without permadeath. A pilot with a small sample suggested permadeath may improve perceptions of play; the author warns it does not generalise. — [TCD SCSS 2014-046 abstract](https://publications.scss.tcd.ie/theses/diss/2014/TCD-SCSS-DISSERTATION-2014-046-ABSTRACT.pdf)
  - Survey of roguelike players on procedural generation, permadeath and persistence. — ["Roguelike Games: The Way We Play," IJEMS 2022](https://doi.org/10.21791/IJEMS.2022.4.7.) (findings not retrieved)
  - MIT CMS thesis on digital death in videogames: [Wilson, "Roguelife"](https://cms.mit.edu/roguelife-digital-death-in-videogames-and-its-design-consequences); literature review lead: Copcic, McKenzie & Hobbs (2013), "Permadeath: A review of literature" (cited in the theses above; not retrieved).

### Inferences
- For a loss to feel learnable, the losing turn should have had a visible better option: the telegraph showed it, a path existed, a Swap/Reroll was available. The study bot could flag fights where *no* line avoided lethal damage. Those are "bad luck" losses, and depth tuning should drive their rate near zero at low depth.
- Following the DST mediation finding, making death meaningful (run-end card, codex, badge progress, loot-die unlock counts) should cushion the frustration of permadeath.
- Choice-of-N rewards, rerolls and swaps all raise autonomy (SDT). Telegraphs and clear feedback raise competence.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed CHI PLAY/FDG/DiGRA study found that specifically measures attribution (skill vs luck) for losses in roguelikes. Searches returned only theses.
- No source found that quantifies how many mitigation tools ("choice of 3", rerolls) players need before RNG losses feel fair.

## 5. Academic work on difficulty, flow, DDA, fiero and frustration

### Takeaway
Flow (challenge-skill balance plus clear goals and immediate feedback) is the dominant framework. Chen's flOw argues for player-chosen rather than invisible difficulty adjustment. Lazzaro's "fiero" needs real frustration first. Newer evidence suggests enjoyment is driven by *learning progress* more than by challenge-skill balance itself. Evidence on DDA's benefits is mixed.

### Cited Findings
- **Flow in games (Csikszentmihalyi via Chen).** Jenova Chen's flOw (2006 Flash thesis game; PS3 version won Best Downloadable Game at the 2008 GDC Awards) was based on his USC research on DDA and Csikszentmihalyi's flow. His approach, "player-oriented active DDA," lets players choose to change difficulty as they go, on the theory that choice improves enjoyment. — [Wikipedia, flOw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(video_game)); [AICS 2017 paper](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2086/AICS2017_paper_32.pdf); [Grenzemann master's thesis](https://uko.opus.hbz-nrw.de/files/1011/Masterarbeit_Scarlett_Grenzemann.pdf)
- **Flow prerequisites are broader than balance.** They include clear goals and immediate feedback; most discussions fixate on "balance between ability level and challenge," and flow can occur without a difficulty curve (e.g., The Sims). — [Game Developer, "Gamification Dynamics: Flow and Art"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gamification-dynamics-flow-and-art)
- **RESEARCH (2025): learning progress drives enjoyment.** "Leveling up fun: learning progress, expectations, and success influence enjoyment in video games": when learning progress was suppressed, the authors found no relationship between difficulty-skill balance and enjoyment. — [PMC12488971](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12488971/)
- **DDA (Hunicke 2005).** "The Case for Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment in Games" (ACM SIGCHI ACE 2005) and the Hamlet system on the Half-Life engine adjust item supply and demand using inventory theory. The aim was to explore DDA requirements without degrading core gameplay. Secondary reviews describe the player results as unclear. — [Hunicke & Chapman, Hamlet PDF](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/pubs/Hamlet.pdf); [Hunicke vitae](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/vitae.html)
- **Fiero (Nicole Lazzaro, Four Keys to Fun; XEODesign player research, practitioner/industry).** Hard Fun yields fiero, the "epic win" from beating a difficult goal. Making things easy "won't get you there"; players need to feel frustrated, then win. Bestselling games tend to offer three of the four keys (Easy, Hard, People, Serious Fun). — [Big Think interview](https://bigthink.com/videos/what-makes-a-game-fun/); [Voices of VR #221](https://voicesofvr.com/nicole-lazzaro-on-the-four-keys-to-fun-in-virtual-reality)
- **Difficulty pacing with peaks motivates more** (see section 2). — [HAL](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-03847459)
- **Juul's failure paradox** (section 4) is the main humanities account of frustration as a component of enjoyment. — [MIT CMS](https://cms.mit.edu/jesper-juul-the-pain-of-playing-video-games)

### Inferences
- The depth system is player-chosen difficulty, which is Chen's preferred form of DDA, and it avoids the trust problems of invisible adjustment. Hidden assist (XCOM-style) is still defensible at low depth, as long as it only ever helps the player (Meier's "winner paradox").
- Bosses with telegraphs plus a real chance of losing are the "Hard Fun" source of fiero. Because the 2025 study links enjoyment to learning progress, boss kits should teach (heralds, previews) and then test, not just raise numbers.
