# Fix unfair enemies, keep wild player power

*Research report for Jeremy, October 13, 2026. Written from the notes in `docs/research/research_notes/Roguelike balance literature review/`.*

> **Read this first: how far to trust the sources.** The research team could not open most web pages in full (the session's network blocked them). Every quote and number below comes from **search-engine excerpts** of the cited pages, not from reading the pages themselves. Quotes are close to the original wording but were not checked against it. Claims the notes could not confirm at all are marked **[UNVERIFIED]**. Several sources are secondary (wikis, summaries, interviews reported by others), and win-rate figures come from achievement trackers with unknown snapshot dates. **Re-check any quote or number against the original before you quote it anywhere else.** The numbers about Dungeon Scramble come from Jeremy's own study files and are bot results, not human results.

**Bottom line.** Roguelike designers broadly agree with Jeremy's Oct 13 philosophy. In a single-player roguelike, a rare overpowered player combo is acceptable and even desirable, because nobody else is hurt by it. Slay the Spire's co-creator listed "Single Player + Rogue-like" as a balance *advantage*, Monster Train's team said it does not aim for a balance without "super powered combos", and Vampire Survivors' creator said "balance is completely out of the window". The places where designers *do* insist on balance are narrower: enemies must be readable and answerable (telegraphs, no unavoidable one-shots), player options that are strong *for free* get tuned because they erase choice, content nobody would pick gets fixed, and difficulty ladders must climb in small, even, legible steps. Dungeon Scramble already fits most of this. Its telegraphs, lethal warning, Floor 1 to 3 death curve and depth ladder (about 4 to 8.5 win points per depth point) match genre practice. Where it differs most is win rate: its bots win **51 to 96%** of base runs, while real players win only about **9%** of Slay the Spire runs at the base level. That gap partly reflects that bots are not humans and partly that Dungeon Scramble is shorter and gentler, so it should be judged with human playtest data before the base game is made harder. The clearest balance work left is on the enemy side (a few Floor 3 damage tails, one under-threatening boss, grindy Floor 2 Easy rooms) and on dead content (the token and die-upgrade shrines), not on player power.

## A few terms, in plain language

These words come up throughout. Most are the genre's jargon; a couple come from game-design theory.

| Term | Plain meaning | Dungeon Scramble example |
|---|---|---|
| **Input randomness** | Luck that happens *before* you decide. You see the result, then plan around it. | Rolling the 4x4 tray, then choosing a path. Which loot dice you are offered. |
| **Output randomness** | Luck that happens *after* you commit. You choose, then a roll decides if it worked. | A hidden "30% chance to stun" on a relic would be this. Dungeon Scramble has almost none. |
| **Variance** | How much results swing from run to run. High variance = some runs crush, some fizzle. | A run that finds Medsack + a heal relic early vs one that finds only Fumble dice. |
| **Telegraph** | Showing the enemy's next move before it happens. | The face-off chips, the boss `herald`, the lethal warning. |
| **Ascension / Heat / Covenant / Stake** | Stacked difficulty levels you unlock by winning. Each adds a small handicap. | **Depth.** |
| **Per-run win rate** | Share of *all runs* that end in a win. | The bot's 73% for the regular profile at Depth 0. |
| **"Ever won" rate** | Share of *players* who have won at least once, over any number of tries (what Steam achievements show). | Not measured yet. |
| **Pick rate / take rate** | When an item is offered, how often it is taken. | Not yet measured for humans; needs telemetry. |
| **Forced-inclusion study** | Make the bot always have item X, compare to the same runs without it. The researchers' name is "restricted play". | The loot-dice and shrine studies. |
| **Dead content** | Items or choices nobody should pick, or that do nothing when picked. | Scroll Dispenser shrines (see below). |
| **One-shot** | Dying in a single enemy turn from healthy. | The study's definition: a fatal turn that started at 50%+ of max HP. |

Three rules fall out of this, and they map almost word for word onto Jeremy's philosophy.

**First, player outliers come in two kinds.** A *committed* outlier pays off a build the player chose and worked for: a rare die plus the right relic. Designers celebrate those. A *universal* outlier is strong with no commitment and gets taken every time: Luck be a Landlord's hypothetical 20-coin Cherry, Monster Train's 3-cost Tome. Those get tuned, not because they win too much, but because they **remove choice**. Risk of Rain 2's fix is gentler than a nerf: move the spiky item to a rarer pool. Balatro does the same with its strongest Jokers, which appear only through a very rare card (about 0.3%, per a guide, not a primary source: [games.gg](https://games.gg/balatro/guides/balatro-jokers-guide/)).

**Second, enemies are made readable more than they are capped.** The fairness tools are telegraphs and guaranteed answers, not low numbers. Slay the Spire's enemy intents are called its "central innovation" because they make it "easy to understand what is about to happen and why" ([LessWrong review](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/MEimkSwwuRgiPaZaY/review-slay-the-spire)). Even so, an enemy that stays too strong does get nerfed (the StS2 elite).

**Third, once players love a strong option, nerfing it is expensive.** The StS2 backlash is the cautionary tale. Get outliers right *before* players attach to them, prefer buffing the alternatives, and use the difficulty ladder to raise the ceiling instead of lowering the floor.

One honest caveat: **no developer in these notes states the asymmetry ("players may be broken, enemies may not") as an explicit rule.** It is a pattern across what they said and did, not a quote. Present it that way if it ever goes into a design document.

## Luck before the choice is the good kind of luck

Most designers prefer input randomness (luck you see, then plan around) to output randomness (luck that decides after you commit). Keith Burgun argues output randomness does not belong in strategy games at all, because a player can play well and still lose to a bad roll, which makes it hard to learn from losses ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/randomness-and-game-design)). The two kinds are a sliding scale, though. Input randomness that leaves the player **too few options to react** causes the same problems as output randomness (same source), and input randomness only works when it comes with real choices ([Level99](https://level99store.com/blogs/guidelines/3-5-1-3-5-2-input-output-randomness)).

The one controlled experiment found in the notes points the other way. In a custom card game, **random card draws (input) lowered satisfaction more than random card effects (output)** ([Zhang et al. 2021, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.08437)). It was a small lab study in one genre and its sample size was not retrieved, so it does not overturn the consensus, but it is a warning: input randomness is not automatically satisfying. It satisfies when the player has tools to act on it.

Dice games show what those tools look like. Dicey Dungeons gives every class its own way to bend the dice. Slice & Dice has heavy output randomness yet feels fair because you can lock good dice and reroll the rest ([Ezra Szanton](https://ezraszanton.substack.com/p/output-randomness-game-designers); the essay's attribution of one quote to Zach Gage should be checked). Even with those tools, one Dicey Dungeons reviewer says "a few unlucky rolls can end a run quickly" ([GamingTrend](https://gamingtrend.com/reviews/of-dice-and-decks-dicey-dungeons-review/)), so luck never fully disappears, and that is accepted.

Dungeon Scramble's core loop (roll the tray, plan a path, resolve it with no further dice) is input randomness with deterministic resolution, the structure the design writers favor. **Its real randomness risk is not output noise but a dead tray**: a roll where no path is any good. Swap, Reroll and the class abilities are exactly the Dicey Dungeons answer to that risk.

Player psychology adds two twists. Sid Meier found that Civilization Revolution testers complained about losing at 3:1 odds in their favor but never about winning at 3:1 against, and accepted losing at 2:1 but not at 20:10 (same ratio, bigger numbers). The team changed the math so it *felt* fair ([GameDev.net notes](https://gamedev.net/blogs/entry/2111213-the-psychology-of-game-design-sid-meier); [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2010/03/12/sid-meier-gamers-arent-logical)). He also observed that players accept unexplained rewards but read unexplained punishments as cheating. Many commercial games quietly bend luck toward the player: Dota 2's "pseudo-random distribution" makes long droughts rare while keeping the average rate ([Liquipedia](https://liquipedia.net/dota2/Random_Number_Generation), community wiki), and XCOM 2 reportedly gives hidden aim help after misses on easier difficulties, with none on the hardest ([Steam discussion quoting the config file](https://steamcommunity.com/app/268500/discussions/0/412447331645399260); exact numbers conflict between sources).

Variance also earns its place through memory. People remember an experience mostly by its **peak and its end**, not its average. A CHI 2016 study that reordered difficulty in otherwise identical games found that remembered challenge followed the peak and end strongly, while fun and willingness to replay followed less consistently ([Gutwin et al.](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~jeromew/data/COMP766/CHI2016/p5608-gutwin.pdf)). That supports Jeremy's "crazy combos are what players remember", with the caution not to over-claim: the effect on *fun* ratings was mixed.

Two points matter for reading this. A Steam achievement percentage is the share of *owners* who ever unlocked it, over any number of tries, so it says nothing about how often a single run wins. And win rate at a high difficulty level measures *who plays there*: StS2's A10 win rate (~17%) is about the same as its A0 rate (~16%) because only skilled players reach A10 ([Two Average Gamers](https://www.twoaveragegamers.com/slay-the-spire-2-beginner-guide/)).

The research notes turn these figures into a rough target for a short 3-floor game: a first-time player loses the first one to three runs, a typical engaged player wins about **20 to 35%** of base runs, and a skilled player wins **60% or more**. These are the researchers' estimates from the comparisons, **not published standards**. Shorter, more forgiving runs (Balatro, Monster Train) do show higher win-once rates than Slay the Spire, which supports aiming above StS1's 9%.

## Difficulty ladders climb in small, even, legible steps

Every major ladder stacks small, readable handicaps and saves a few rule changes for milestones. Hades' Pact of Punishment uses steps like +20% enemy damage per rank, −25% healing per rank and +40% shop prices per rank, with new boss moves at the top ([Prima Games](https://primagames.com/gaming/hades-guide-pact-of-punishment-modifiers-heat); [RPG Site](https://www.rpgsite.net/feature/10287-hades-pact-of-punishment-heat-modifiers-and-how-to-maximize-your-rewards)). Slay the Spire's ladder adds more elites early, a starting curse in the middle and a double final boss at the top ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/slay-the-spire-2/ascension-levels)). Several steps in every system tax **recovery and economy** (healing, gold, prices), not just enemy strength, which makes planning tighter instead of just making fights longer.

Two lessons from player reactions stand out. Monster Train changed one Covenant step from "Dazzle" (your units can't act) to "Emberdrain" (−1 energy next turn) after players found it frustrating ([Steam forum](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1102190/discussions/0/3092275748073975130)): **handicaps that take away actions feel worse than handicaps that tax resources.** And Hades' top Heat (64) was set beyond normal human reach on purpose; a reported 64-Heat clear was news ([Prima Games](https://primagames.com/news/an-amazing-player-just-completed-a-seemingly-impossible-hades-run)). The top of a ladder is allowed to be near-impossible. Hades also pays rewards only for beating a new personal-best Heat, which pulls players up the ladder.

Qualitative boss changes are hard to balance evenly. Hades players found one reworked boss easier and another "a menace" ([Steam forum](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1145360/discussions/2/1745646405806420097)), so new moves need their own check.

## Fair enemies are telegraphed, answerable and rarely one-shot you

The genre's main fairness tool is near-perfect information about the next enemy action, so a loss can be traced to a decision. Into the Breach was built so "every death felt like your own fault" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-subset-games-i-into-the-breach-i-)). StS2 shows multiple intents with actual numbers so players have "a baseline understanding of what is coming at all times" ([untapped.gg guide](https://sts2.untapped.gg/guides/how-to-read-enemy-intent)). The often-repeated claim that Into the Breach's designer said every loss should be the player's fault, in exactly those words at GDC, is **[UNVERIFIED]**.

On one-shots, the practitioner rule is that a character should not die from a single attack under reasonable play, and that lethal moments must be "recognizable and escapable" ([design notes citing a book chapter that was not checked](https://kn86-docs-gca3d.kinsta.page/software/cartridges/authoring/roguelike-design/)). Cogmind's developer adds: give the player enough information and escape options that a resourceful player always has several responses ([r/roguelikedev](https://redlib.ssps.io/r/roguelikedev/comments/3hs9gi/faq_friday_19_permadeath)). **No studio has published a numeric cap** on damage per hit. The notes' working heuristic (avoid enemy turns above about 50 to 60% of the player's max HP at base depth unless they are telegraphed with an answer available) is the researchers' inference, not an industry number.

Academic work explains why this matters. Jesper Juul's *The Art of Failure* argues that players willingly seek failure when it reads as their own, fixable shortcoming ([MIT CMS](https://cms.mit.edu/jesper-juul-the-pain-of-playing-video-games)). Self-determination research found that feeling competent and in control predicts enjoyment ([ScienceDaily on Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/12/061226134706.htm)). A 2025 study found enjoyment tracks **learning progress** more than a perfect challenge-skill match ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12488971/)). A small Don't Starve Together study (n=65) found permadeath raises frustration, but how *meaningful* the death felt decided whether people kept playing ([NCCU thesis](https://thesis.lib.nccu.edu.tw/thesis/detail/b6a7e5c99b153b3ae67e5b7ab41e1f11)). In short: a hard loss you can learn from is fine; a loss that came from nowhere is what drives players off.

## Outliers and dead content need controlled tests, not raw win rates

The core metrics used by Mega Crit, Shiny Shoe and the card-game data sites are **pick rate**, **win rate of runs holding the item**, and **HP lost per encounter**. Monster Train's dashboards tracked "median player health lost in a given battle" alongside card pick rates and clan-combination win rates ([Film Stories](https://filmstories.co.uk/gaming/monster-train-literally-a-train-full-of-monsters/), via search summary). Everyone warns that these numbers are easy to collect and hard to read.

"Win rate when held" is misleading in three ways. Good players pick certain items, so the item inherits their skill. Items found late look strong simply because the run survived long enough to find them. And items that show up in already-strong builds look strong by association ([StarCityGames on 17Lands](https://articles.starcitygames.com/2021/03/25/using-17lands-com-as-a-resource-to-improve-at-limited/); [MetaBot](https://metabot.gg/en/slay-the-spire-2/cards/win-rate)). The fix is a controlled test: force the item in, compare against matched runs without it. Researchers call this "restricted play" ([Jaffe et al., AIIDE](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12513)), and it is exactly what Jeremy's loot-dice and shrine studies already do. Using the same seed list for both arms cuts noise further.

Bots have known limits. Studies of simulated players recommend testing at **several skill levels**, because an item's value can flip with skill ([Zook et al., Cardonomicon](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~riedl/pubs/zook-fdg15.pdf), via summary). A community Slay the Spire bot gained 11 floors on average just from better drafting and map choices with the same combat engine ([Hugging Face model card](https://huggingface.co/Jialeiv/sts-rl-agent), not peer-reviewed, n=50): **the bot's choice policy shapes its results as much as its fighting does.** A bot that cannot use an item's trick will undervalue it. And bots cannot measure clarity, tedium or "feels bad".

On dead content, the sources do not ask for equal power. Mega Crit's rule is that every card should have a *purpose* (via [summary](https://glasp.co/youtube/7rqfbvnO_H0)). The notes combine the evidence into a two-signal test: an item that is **rarely taken when offered and also shows near-zero effect when forced in** is truly dead. Rarely taken but positive when forced means players misread it, so fix the text. Often taken but zero or negative when forced is a **trap**, so buff it or make its weakness visible. Situational items are fine if their situation actually happens often enough.

On mobile, general benchmarks put median sessions at about **5 minutes** and top games at **8 to 9 minutes** ([GameAnalytics 2025](https://investgame.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2025-GameAnalytics-Mobile-Gaming-Benchmarks.pdf)). No roguelike-specific mobile data on losing streaks or retention was found.

| Telegraphing | StS/ITB: show the next move with real numbers | Next-turn chips, herald, lethal warning with D.O.T. | **Fits.** One gap: a damage-per-Minion special's chip reads as a rate ("3/Minion"), not a total; StS2 shows the number. |
| Boss parity | Hades players noticed one reworked boss being far harsher than another | Expert Depth 7 boss threat 2.2% (Extruzeon) to 18.9% (Necrorancher) | **Mixed.** Extruzeon under-threatens skilled players; most others sit within about 10 to 19%. |
| Player outliers | Committed outliers fine; universal ones tuned | Medsack +16 vs Alloy median +8 after tweak (was +20); sustain and Block lead every tier | **Mostly fits.** Medsack is the "free and universal" type, but already softened. The "sustain wins every tier" pattern is the thing to watch, not any one die. |
| Dead content | Every item should have a purpose | After the six identity tweaks no loot die is far below its tier median; Scroll Dispensers −18 to −27, Enchanted Anvil −13, loot-relic shrines +1/−8 (amplified method) | **Misfit on shrines.** Token and die-upgrade shrines are dead or traps. |
| Tedium | Balatro eased high-stake scaling because it was tedious | Floor 2 Easy rooms 4.5 turns (slower than Standard's 3.4) but cheap in HP; Myceleus 7.7 turns | **Misfit.** These read as grind. The study's proposed HP cuts match genre practice. |
| Output randomness | Avoid or make it visible and controllable | Path resolution is deterministic; real Minion faces revealed only during resolution (anti-scouting) | **Fits**, with one watch-item: a hidden face under a Minion is a small piece of after-commit luck. A blank landing ends the path. Keep it readable. |

The single biggest open question is the first row. If human players win as often as the regular bot (73%), Dungeon Scramble's base game is far more generous than Slay the Spire, closer to the "ever won" rates of Balatro and Monster Train 2 than to anyone's per-run rate. That is not automatically wrong: the run is short, the first win unlocks the depth menu, and depth is where the genre puts the real difficulty. But it means **the base game should not be judged "too easy" or "too hard" from bot numbers alone.**

## Guidelines for Dungeon Scramble

These guidelines turn the research into checks Jeremy can run with the headless harness and on his phone. None of them changes a current number. Where a study already proposed a fix, the guideline says whether the literature supports it.

### Where balance is needed

| # | Guideline | Why (from the research) | Bot check | Phone / telemetry check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Every enemy turn that can kill must be visible a turn ahead, as a number.** | StS intents, ITB telegraphs; Meier: unexplained punishment reads as cheating. | Already logged: worst single turn, fatal-turn starting HP. | When a damage-per-Minion special is next, check that the chip or tooltip makes the *total* obvious (e.g. "3/Minion = 15"), not just the rate. |
| 2 | **Keep one-shots rare and answerable.** Working line: at Depth 0, an enemy turn above ~50 to 60% of the player's max HP should be heralded *and* have a counter in most trays. | One-shot rule; Cogmind; the 50 to 60% figure is a heuristic, not a standard. | Keep "one-shot share of deaths" below its current 2 to 3% at Depth 0. For the 45 to 69 tails, log whether any legal path that turn would have prevented death. Deaths with *no* surviving path are "bad luck" deaths; push those near zero at low depth. | Note any death that felt like it came from nowhere. If the Floor 3 tails do, the study's guard (count at most 8 Minions, cap Extruzeon's layers) fits genre practice. |
| 3 | **Fix enemies that are outliers in either direction.** | StS2 nerfed an elite; Hades players flagged uneven reworked bosses. | Boss threat per profile. Flag any boss more than about 2x or less than 0.5x the median for its floor at a given profile. Today that flags Extruzeon at expert depth (2.2% vs 10 to 19%). The study's idea (more HP, not bigger hits, so layers stack) keeps its identity. | Does Extruzeon feel like a Big Boss when you play it well? |
| 4 | **Remove tedium.** Fights that cost little but take long are the problem, not fights that are hard. | Balatro eased tedious scaling; the Snecko Eye complaint was annoyance, not power. | Turns per fight vs HP lost per fight by room. Flag rooms slower than the next tier up. Today: Floor 2 Easy rooms and Myceleus. The study's HP cuts fit. | Time per fight from the active-time clock. |
| 5 | **Fix dead content and traps.** | "Every card should have a purpose"; two-signal dead-content test. | Forced-inclusion delta per item, compared within its tier or floor, with run counts shown. Today: Scroll Dispensers, Enchanted Anvil, Expediting Spores, loot-relic shrines. Re-test any fix at normal shrine frequency, since the shrine study amplifies effects. | Once testers send data: take rate when offered. Taken often + negative bot delta = trap (fix first). Rarely taken + positive delta = unclear text. |
| 6 | **Tune "free and universal" player power, not "earned" power.** | Luck be a Landlord's Cherry; Monster Train's Tome; StS "nothing too warping". | Flag an item if it is near the top of its tier *and* strong for every class/origin *and* needs no setup. Medsack is the only current case and was already softened. | Is a die taken every time it is offered, crowding out other choices? |
| 7 | **Keep the depth ladder even, and prefer taxes to lockouts.** | Hades/StS small steps; Monster Train's Dazzle rework. | Keep win points per depth point in the current −4 to −8.5 band. Flag any modifier more than ~2 points cheaper than its neighbors (today: Swarming 2). | Watch whether any modifier makes turns feel unplayable (e.g. Swarming covering most of the tray) rather than just harder. |
| 8 | **Guard against dead trays.** | Input randomness with no viable reaction behaves like output randomness. | Count turns where the best legal path deals 0 damage and gives 0 Block, with Swap/Reroll used. If it is more than rare at Depth 0, consider Dota-style bad-luck protection that only helps the player, generous at low depth and absent at the top. | Phone sessions: how often a tray felt hopeless. |

### Where balance is not needed

| # | Guideline | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | **Leave committed combos broken.** A rare die plus the right relic that wins a fight in one turn is a feature. If one is too frequent, move it to a rarer tier or behind a cost before considering a nerf. | Giovannetti, Shiny Shoe, Galante; Risk of Rain 2's separate pools; peak-end memory. |
| 10 | **Do not aim for equal item strength.** Tiers are *supposed* to differ, and within a tier a spread of roughly ±5 points around the median (about the study's noise) is fine as long as each item has a niche. | Mega Crit's "purpose, not equality"; Monster Train's "not perfect balance". |
| 11 | **Do not chase class or origin parity for skilled play.** Origin explains ~3% of the spread and Jeremy has accepted the class spread. Keep teaching (Skill coach) as the tool. | Skill-dependent values are normal (Cardonomicon); StS2 characters still differ by ~7 points. |
| 12 | **Accept runs that fizzle.** A run that "peters out on bad loot" is part of the genre, as long as the loss is readable. | Juul's failure paradox; Dicey Dungeons reviews; StS1's ~9% base win rate. |
| 13 | **Do not tune the base game from bot win rates alone.** Bot win rates are a ruler for comparing builds, not a forecast. Make the base game harder or easier only after human data (Jeremy's runs plus testers) shows the gap. | Bots measure what they can exploit, not what humans perceive; StS2's A10 vs A0 shows how player population changes win rates. |
| 14 | **Do not add a damage preview for the player's own path** beyond what fairness needs. | LocalThunk: watching your engine run without knowing the result is the fun. The lethal warning is about the *enemy's* turn, which is the fairness case. |

### What to measure next

The bot already covers most of what the literature recommends. The gaps are on the human side, and the research suggests a short list that fits the existing Export Play Data:

| Measure | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Per-floor funnel for humans** (reached F2, reached F3, won) at Depth 0 | The genre's clearest shape metric (Hades 83% to 45%). | Compare directly with the bot's "runs ending on each floor" table. |
| **Human win rate at Depth 0 over a player's first 10 runs** | Lets Jeremy see where real players sit against the researchers' 20 to 35% estimate and the bot's 51 to 73%. | Also record the run number of a player's first win. |
| **Take rate when offered** for loot dice, relics and shrines | Half of the dead-content test; bots can't give it. | Always show the count next to the rate; most per-item human numbers will be noise for a long time. |
| **Death attribution:** killer, whether it was previewed, whether a surviving path existed | Separates "hard" from "unfair" deaths. | Can be computed after the fact from the per-turn tray and path telemetry once the replay tool exists. |
| **Where sessions end vs where runs end** | Mobile sessions are 5 to 9 minutes; a run is ~58 turns for the regular bot. | Quit points between floors are healthy; quit points mid-fight in grindy rooms are not. |
| **Bot studies at two or more skill levels** | Item values can flip with skill. | Already done with expert/regular/new; keep doing it for new content. Note where the bot can't use an item's trick (Swap-heavy dice), so a low score there is a lead, not a verdict. |

## Conclusion

The research mostly confirms Jeremy's instincts and sharpens one of them. "Over-strong enemies are bad" is really about **readability**: designers accept frightening enemies as long as the threat is shown, has an answer and does not come from nowhere. That turns enemy balance into a checkable question (could any legal path have survived?) instead of a matter of taste, and Dungeon Scramble's per-turn telemetry is already rich enough to answer it. Likewise, "over-strong player pieces are fine" holds for *earned* power. The one kind of player power the genre does tune is the free, always-correct pick, because it quietly deletes choices.

The surprise is how far Dungeon Scramble's bot win rates sit from real-world roguelike numbers. That gap is not evidence that the game is too easy; it is evidence that the bots are not people. The most valuable next balance data is therefore not another bot study but a small set of human runs on the phone, read through the same funnel and death-attribution lenses the bot already uses.
